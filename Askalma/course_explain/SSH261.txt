### Course Overview  
**Course Code:** SSH261  
**Course Name:** Democracy in India: Principles and Practices  
**Offered To:** Undergraduate Students  

This course provides an in-depth exploration of the foundational principles of Indian democracy, examining how these principles have shaped contemporary political debates. The course is structured around three core constitutional principles—Justice, Liberty, and Equality—and their implications on key issues such as reservation, secularism, and poverty eradication. Designed to foster critical thinking, the course encourages students to analyze historical, ethical, and policy-related contexts while engaging with primary texts and secondary readings.

---

### Prerequisites  
There are no specific prerequisites for this course. However, a basic understanding of Indian history and political systems is recommended to fully grasp the material.

---

### Learning Outcomes (COs)  
By the end of this course, students will be able to:  

1. **Understand** the dominant approaches to conceptualizing Indian democracy.  
2. **Analyze** the historical and ethical roots of Indian democracy in the context of the independence movement.  
3. **Grasp** the relationship between constitutional principles, political debates, and policy-making.  
4. **Evaluate** contentious political debates within their constitutional, historical, and political contexts.  
5. **Assess** and understand the functioning of Indian democracy through various lenses.

---

### Weekly Syllabus and Topics  

#### Week 1: Evolution of the Constitutional Principles  
- Introduction to Indian democracy and its foundational principles (CO-1, CO-2).  
- Overview of the evolution of constitutional principles in India.  

#### Week 2: Conceptual Tenets of Indian Democracy  
- Justice as a constitutional principle (CO-1, CO-3, CO-4).  
- Freedom as a constitutional principle (CO-1, CO-2, CO-3).  
- Equality as a constitutional principle (CO-1, CO-2, CO-3).  

#### Week 3: Justice and the Reservation Question  
- Detailed analysis of the reservation policy within the framework of justice.  
- Assignments related to this topic.  

#### Week 4: Justice and the Secularism Question  
- Examination of secularism as a constitutional principle (CO-1, CO-3, CO-4).  

#### Week 5: Justice and the Poverty Question  
- Linking justice with poverty eradication (CO-2, CO-3, CO-4, CO-5).  

#### Week 6: Freedom and the Secularism Question  
- Exploring secularism as a state policy (CO-1, CO-3, CO-4).  
- Assignments related to this topic.  

#### Week 7: Freedom and the Secularism Question  
- Further analysis of secularism as a guarantee of freedom (CO-1, CO-3, CO-4, CO-5).  
- Presentations on assigned topics.  

#### Week 8: Equality and the Poverty Question  
- Understanding equality as a constitutional principle (CO-2, CO-3, CO-4).  
- Assignments related to this topic.  

#### Week 9: Equality and the Poverty Question  
- Linking poverty eradication with equality (CO-2, CO-3, CO-4, CO-5).  

#### Week 10: Equality and the Poverty Question  
- Case studies on poverty eradication as a policy.  

#### Week 11: Evaluating Indian Democracy  
- Comprehensive evaluation of contemporary issues in Indian democracy (CO-2, CO-3, CO-4, CO-5).  

#### Week 12: Revisions and Reflections  
- Revisiting key concepts and reflecting on the course journey (CO-1, CO-2, CO-3, CO-4, CO-5).  
- Response papers due.

### Assessment Strategy  
The final grade is determined through a combination of assessments:  
- **Assignments:** 10% (Evaluate understanding of key concepts and policies).  
- **Response Papers:** 25% (Reflect on critical issues in Indian democracy).  
- **Presentations:** 20% (Demonstrate analytical skills and ability to communicate ideas).  
- **Mid-Semester Examination:** 20% (Assess foundational knowledge and understanding of course material).  
- **Final Examination:** 25% (Comprehensive evaluation of overall learning outcomes).  

The mid-semester and final exams carry significant weightage as they assess the students' ability to synthesize and apply their knowledge across diverse contexts.

### Learning Resources  
1. **Sumit Sarkar, “Indian Democracy: The Historical Origins”** by Atul Kohli (2001), Cambridge University Press.  
2. **Neera Chandhoke and Rajesh Kumar, “Indian Democracy: Cognitive Maps”** by K C Suri/Achin Vanaik (eds) (2013), Oxford University Press.  
3. **Rajeev Bhargava, Introduction: Outline of a Political Theory of the Indian Constitution**, available online.  
4. **Tania Burchardt and Gary Craig, “Introduction”** in *Social Justice in Public Policy: Seeking Fairness in Diverse Societies* (2008), Bristol University Press.  
5. **Vinay Sitapathi, “Reservation”** in *The Oxford Handbook of the Indian Constitution*, available online.  
6. **Ronojoy Sen, “Secularism and Religious Freedom”** in *The Oxford Handbook of the Indian Constitution*, available online.  
7. **Neera Chandhoke, “Secularism”** in *The Oxford Companion to Politics in India*, available online.  
8. **Christophe Jaffralot, “The Fate of Secularism in India”**, Carnegie Endowment Fund for International Peace, available online.  
9. **Gautam Bhatia, “The Transformative Constitution: A Radical Biography”** (2019), Harper Collins India.  
10. **Stuart Corbridge, John Harriss, and Craig Jefferey, “How Have the Poor Faired? (And Others Too)”** in *India Today: Economy, Politics, Society* (2013), Polity Press.  
11. **Partha Chatterjee, “Democracy and Economic Transformation in India”**, Economic and Political Weekly, Volume 43, Number 16 (2008).  

Additional readings will be provided weekly to supplement the course material.
