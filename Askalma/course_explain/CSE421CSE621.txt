### Course Overview  
The course titled "Complexity Theory" (Course Code: CSE421/CSE621) is a 4-credit module offered to both undergraduate and postgraduate students. This course provides an in-depth exploration of computational problems from the perspective of computational resources, such as time, space, circuit size, and depth. It examines how these resources influence problem-solving difficulty and their relationships with different models of computation, including non-deterministic, non-uniform, and randomized models. The course also delves into the classification of problems into complexity classes and their implications for algorithm design, cryptography, and theoretical computer science.

### Prerequisites  
The prerequisite structure for this course is as follows:  
- **Mandatory Prerequisites:**  
  - CSE102 (Discrete Mathematics)  
  - MTH201 (Calculus)  
  - CSE322 (Data Structures and Algorithms)  
  - CSE121 (Introduction to Programming)  

These courses provide the foundational knowledge necessary for understanding the advanced concepts covered in Complexity Theory. Students are expected to have a strong grasp of logical reasoning, algorithmic thinking, and mathematical proofs before enrolling in this course.

- **Desirable Prerequisites:**  
  - Exposure to theoretical computer science topics such as automata theory or formal languages is recommended but not mandatory.  

### Course Outcomes (COs)  
By the end of this course, students will be able to:  
1. **CO1:** Demonstrate a deep understanding of different models of computation and their interrelationships.  
2. **CO2:** Analyze and bound the resource usage required for solving computational problems under various models of computation.  
3. **CO3:** Classify problems into appropriate complexity classes and understand their hierarchical relationships.  
4. **CO4:** Apply techniques to place problems in complexity class hierarchies, including demonstrating separations, collapses, or completeness.  
5. **CO5:** Articulate the major unresolved questions in computational complexity, such as P vs NP, and recognize their significance in computer science.

### Syllabus  
The course is structured over 12 weeks, with each week focusing on specific topics:  

- **Week 1:** Introduction to computational problems and models of computation.  
- **Week 2:** Turing machines and time/space complexity measures.  
- **Week 3:** Universal machine and the relationship between deterministic and non-deterministic classes.  
- **Week 4:** Complexity class hierarchies (P, NP, PSPACE, EXP, LOG, NLOG).  
- **Week 5:** Complete problems in these classes.  
- **Week 6:** Circuits and non-uniform models of computation (AC, NC).  
- **Week 7:** Randomized algorithms and complexity classes.  
- **Week 8:** Oracle results and advanced topics.  

The course concludes with a flipped classroom model, emphasizing the importance of logical reasoning and maturity in understanding advanced theoretical concepts.

### Assessment Strategy  
The course is evaluated through a combination of quizzes, midsem, homework, and endsem:  
- **Quizzes (25%):** Weekly quizzes to assess understanding of weekly topics.  
- **Midsem Exam (30%):** Covers the first half of the syllabus, emphasizing problem-solving and theoretical analysis.  
- **Homework Assignments (25%):** Bi-weekly assignments that include both theoretical proofs and algorithm design problems.  
- **Endsem Exam (20%):** Comprehensive examination assessing overall understanding of the course material.

### Resources  
The primary textbook for this course is "Complexity Theory: A Modern Approach" by Sanjeev Arora and Boaz Barak, supplemented by "Introduction to the Theory of Computation" by Michael Sipser. Additional resources include lecture notes and supplementary readings provided during class.  

This structured approach ensures a comprehensive understanding of computational complexity theory, preparing students for advanced research and applications in theoretical computer science, cryptography, and algorithm design.
